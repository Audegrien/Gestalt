GESTALT — PROJECT MASTER BRIEF & AI COLLABORATION INSTRUCTIONS

Hi, I'm Alfie, and I'm developing a game called GESTALT with Noah. This document explains the project's identity, design philosophy, mechanics, visual direction, technical requirements, and how I want you to help us develop it.

Your job is to become a reliable development partner who understands the whole project, not just an AI that generates code on request.

============================================================
1. PROJECT OVERVIEW
============================================================

Title: GESTALT
Engine: GameMaker Studio 2 / GameMaker
Genre: Top-down psychological horror RPG
Combat: Turn-based, inspired by Undertale and Deltarune
Presentation: Dark pixel art, environmental storytelling, psychological distortion, oppressive atmosphere.

GESTALT explores psychological horror through gameplay, atmosphere, character behaviour, symbolism, and the player's perception of the world.

The central design ambition is to create a game in which the horror is not simply something the player sees. It is something they experience through the mechanics. The player's mental state should affect how they interpret the world, how dangerous encounters become, and how the game presents itself.

Major influences:
- Cry of Fear: vulnerability, oppressive environments, disturbing imagery and psychological unease.
- Silent Hill: symbolism, environmental storytelling, distorted reality and the relationship between psychological states and physical spaces.
- Fear & Hunger: danger, vulnerability, consequential decisions and encounters that cannot always be solved by fighting.
- Undertale and Deltarune: readable turn-based combat, distinctive enemy personalities, memorable interactions and mechanical meaning behind player choices.

These are influences, not templates. GESTALT needs its own identity, characters, mechanics, imagery and atmosphere.

============================================================
2. DESIGN PHILOSOPHY
============================================================

The most important principle is psychological horror through interaction.

The game should not depend entirely on jump scares, gore or monsters appearing unexpectedly. It should create unease through the environment, uncertainty, sound, unusual patterns, uncomfortable choices and the gradual loss of confidence in what the player perceives.

The world should feel believable at first. Its horror becomes more effective when familiar places, objects, colours and behaviours begin to feel subtly wrong.

We want:
- Meaningful mechanics rather than mechanics that exist only because they are familiar to RPGs.
- Enemies that communicate something through their behaviour and design.
- Exploration that rewards observation and makes the environment feel connected.
- Consequences that affect the player's experience without always explaining themselves.
- A consistent identity across gameplay, UI, sound, environments and narrative.
- Restraint. An unsettling detail used carefully is often more effective than constant visual noise.

Do not make the game generically spooky. Every major feature should support its psychological themes.

3. STORY AND SETTING
============================================================

The current narrative foundation involves Henry, an ex-police officer, whose wife dies in a car crash. The story and its finer details are still being developed, so do not treat this premise as a complete or final script.

A central location is Mereview Hospital, a secretive therapy facility. The hospital should feel like a believable institution with an unsettling underlying purpose.

The setting should support environmental storytelling. Rooms, documents, objects, architecture, patient records and the behaviour of people or creatures should suggest a larger reality without explaining everything outright.

Important narrative rules:
- Treat unfinished story details as provisional.
- Do not invent major plot revelations, character histories or endings and present them as established canon.
- You may propose new ideas, but clearly label them as suggestions.
- Preserve ambiguity where it strengthens the horror.
- Do not write the entire story before understanding the existing direction.
- Do not assume that every enemy is a literal manifestation of a particular diagnosis or psychological condition.

The narrative is a collaborative work in progress. Help us develop it without taking ownership of decisions that belong to us.

4. CORE MENTAL-STATE SYSTEM
============================================================

A central gameplay variable is:

global.mental_state

The intended range is 0–100, with 100 representing the upper end of the scale.

Mental state is more than a conventional health bar. It is a foundational system that influences the player's experience of the game.

It may influence:
- World presentation and visual distortions.
- Enemy difficulty and encounter behaviour.
- Environmental events.
- The atmosphere and interpretation of familiar spaces.
- Combat decisions and their consequences.
- The availability or outcome of certain narrative paths.

The exact effects should be designed carefully rather than implemented indiscriminately.

THE BROKEN WORLD

When mental state falls below 50, the game enters a more hostile, distorted state referred to as the broken world.

In this state:
- Enemies become substantially more dangerous.
- Henry becomes weaker.
- Encounters remain possible, but fighting is discouraged.
- The world can become more disturbing and less trustworthy.

The intention is to create a meaningful feedback loop: careless or aggressive play can make future situations harder, while thoughtful decisions can help the player preserve their ability to cope.

Do not automatically turn low mental state into a simple game-over condition. It should change the experience and create consequences.

When implementing this system, centralise shared rules where practical, avoid contradictory thresholds, and check for accidental repeated triggers when crossing the 50-point boundary.

============================================================
5. TURN-BASED COMBAT
============================================================

Combat draws inspiration from Undertale and Deltarune, but its central theme is psychological confrontation rather than simply defeating enemies.

FIGHT
A direct, aggressive approach. FIGHT lowers mental state and can make subsequent encounters more dangerous. It should remain a valid option, but the player should understand that violence has consequences.

CONFRONT
A key mechanic that raises mental state and allows the player to engage with an enemy in a more meaningful way. Each enemy should have its own distinct confrontation sequence, behaviour or set of steps. The player must understand the particular encounter rather than relying on one universal solution.

Correctly completing an enemy's confrontation can lead to MOVE ON, allowing the encounter to conclude without simply defeating the enemy through violence.

CONFRONT is important to reaching the good ending.

ITEMS
Items provide additional tactical options. They should have understandable effects and, where appropriate, thematic significance.

FLEE
The player must have the option to flee. Not every encounter needs to be won, and retreat should be considered a legitimate survival decision.

COMBAT PRESENTATION AND FLOW
The intended battle experience is a dedicated, enemy-focused screen. Do not allow ordinary overworld movement during the attack phase. The combat sequence should have a clear separation between the player's command phase and the enemy's attack phase.

The visual and mechanical flow should be understandable, even when the encounter itself is psychologically unsettling.

COMBAT IMPLEMENTATION RULES
- Each enemy should have distinct data and confrontation behaviour.
- Avoid hardcoding every enemy into one enormous conditional statement.
- Keep enemy data, combat state, turn progression and presentation reasonably separated.
- Make sure turn transitions cannot accidentally execute twice.
- Check that enemies cannot receive commands or read variables before their data is initialised.
- Ensure FIGHT, CONFRONT, ITEMS, MOVE ON and FLEE have clearly defined state transitions.
- Do not implement a new combat architecture without first inspecting the existing project.

============================================================
6. LIGHT AND EXPLORATION
============================================================

The game includes an Ultimate Light System intended to make illumination a resource and an atmospheric mechanic.

Current design targets:
- Approximately one minute of usable light.
- Approximately two seconds for the recharge cycle.

These are design targets, not immutable balance values.

The light system should create tension through limited visibility and resource management. Lighting should help communicate the environment while also making darkness meaningful.

Avoid making the light merely a decorative flashlight. Consider how it interacts with exploration, enemy encounters and the player's sense of safety.

WORLD STRUCTURE
The world is intended to include interconnected hospital interiors and a semi-open outdoor area based on our existing map planning.

Rooms and areas should connect through explicit transitions or warp points, with invisible collision where needed to keep the player within intended boundaries.

Priorities include:
- Consistent player movement and collision.
- Predictable room transitions.
- Clear boundaries between walkable and non-walkable areas.
- Reusable approaches to doors, entrances and exits.
- Environments that feel connected rather than like unrelated levels.
- Exploration that rewards attention to environmental details.

Do not replace the existing map structure or invent a new room-management system without reviewing the current implementation first.

7. ART DIRECTION AND VISUAL IDENTITY
============================================================

GESTALT uses dark pixel art with deliberate colour economy. Undertale and Deltarune demonstrate how a restricted palette can create recognisable characters and clear presentation, while the horror influences provide a darker environmental foundation.

The target is generally a restrained palette of around 6–8 principal colours, with variation where required for readability, lighting and important visual effects.

The art should feel:
- Gritty, oppressive and psychologically unsettling.
- Readable at actual gameplay scale.
- Intentional in its use of contrast.
- Grounded in believable environments.
- Distinctive without relying on generic horror colour schemes.

Avoid defaulting to the familiar purple-and-teal or purple-and-mauve horror aesthetic. We want a less predictable visual language.

COLOUR CONCEPT: “THE COLOUR THAT DOESN'T BELONG”

A developing visual direction uses an unnatural colour that appears in places where it should not be. It begins as a subtle anomaly and gradually becomes harder to ignore.

Potential palette anchors:
- False Mint: #D8E0A3
- Memory Pink: #E7A4A1
- Afterimage Blue: #667FD1
- False Sun: #E8C84E
- Old Wound: #704D48
- Unremembered: #252337

These are proposed visual anchors, not a requirement to force every asset into the same six colours.

A recurring false-sun motif may appear across unrelated environments, objects, interfaces and enemies. As mental state deteriorates, the motif could intrude more heavily into the world.

Treat this as a developing art direction and help refine it consistently rather than inventing a new palette for every screen.

GENERAL ASSET SCALE
Existing design discussions have included:
- 16×16 tiles and small overworld assets.
- Player and overworld characters around 24–32 pixels, depending on the asset.
- Enemies around 64×64 pixels for relevant presentation.
- Character portraits around 128×128 pixels.

These sizes are not universal rules. Inspect the actual project and asset conventions before creating replacements.

8. USER INTERFACE
============================================================

The UI should feel like part of the game's world, not a generic RPG menu layered over it.

An established concept is a psychological scrapbook or patient-file interface, using paper textures, clinical documentation, restrained colours and unsettling details.

Possible elements include:
- A full-screen, 16:9 composition.
- Paper-like panels and document textures.
- Top-level navigation tabs.
- Pause, inventory and stats presented cohesively.
- A pixel portrait of Henry.
- Patient-chart-inspired statistics.
- Item descriptions and contextual information.
- Small visual changes that reflect declining mental state.

The UI should prioritise readability and ease of use while subtly communicating the player's condition.

Do not add visual distortion simply to make the interface look horror-themed. Every effect should have a clear purpose and should not make essential information impossible to read.

9. TECHNICAL DEVELOPMENT IN GAMEMAKER
============================================================

This is an active GameMaker project, not a blank-slate programming exercise.

Your first responsibility when asked to change code is to understand the existing implementation.

Before proposing substantial changes:
1. Inspect the relevant objects, scripts, events, rooms and data structures.
2. Identify how the existing system works.
3. Trace dependencies and find where variables are created, modified and read.
4. Identify the smallest safe change that solves the problem.
5. Explain which files or events need to change.
6. Check how the change interacts with existing systems.

Do not assume that a variable, script, object or event exists just because it would be convenient.

GAMEMAKER-SPECIFIC REQUIREMENTS
- Use valid GameMaker Language (GML) for the project's version.
- Identify the correct object and event for each code change.
- Distinguish between Create, Step, Draw, collision and other events.
- Explain required object variables and their initialisation.
- Preserve existing naming conventions and architecture where practical.
- Do not silently replace working systems with simplified examples.
- Check for undefined variables, incorrect instance references, scope problems and invalid array or struct access.
- Consider room transitions, object persistence, instance creation and destruction.
- Avoid expensive operations running unnecessarily every Step event.
- Keep changes small and testable.
- When providing code, state whether it replaces an entire event or should be inserted into an existing script.

A previous development issue involved an enemy object attempting to read its enemy ID before that variable had been set. This illustrates why initialisation order and data flow matter. Do not assume that issue is still present; inspect the current code if it becomes relevant.

WHEN DEBUGGING
1. Explain what the error means in plain English.
2. Identify the likely cause using the actual error message and available code.
3. Ask for only the relevant missing code or files.
4. Provide a specific fix.
5. Explain exactly where to apply it.
6. Describe a short test that verifies the fix.
7. Consider whether the fix could break another system.

Do not respond to every error by rewriting the whole feature.
