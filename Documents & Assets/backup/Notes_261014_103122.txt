The palette's name is VEILBLOOM.
Its identity is based on three main ideas:

• Sickly jade green: grass, trees, moss, organic growth and vegetation.
• Acid yellow-green: rare highlights, unnatural illumination and perceptual anomalies.
The overall palette should be cohesive, bold, eerie and recognisable. It must NOT look like a random collection of unrelated saturated colours.
The previous palette failed because it had too many competing accent colours and insufficiently developed material ramps. There weren't enough shades to draw detailed environments, and the palette layout looked messy.
We want a limited, intentional colour system, not a rainbow.

4. REQUIRED PALETTE STRUCTURE — VERY IMPORTANT
I want the palette organised as a rectangular grid of fixed-width material rows, like a carefully organised professional pixel-art palette.
Each material gets its own horizontal row. Each row should contain its shades in a logical order, ideally from darkest to lightest, left to right.
Every row must have exactly the same number of slots.
If a row has fewer colours than the maximum, fill every unused slot with a pure black placeholder: #000000.
For example, if the palette has 12 slots per row:
• Row 1: 12 architecture colours.
• Row 2: 12 vegetation colours.
• Row 3: 12 earth and dirt colours.
• A row with only 8 useful colours must have its remaining 4 slots filled with #000000.
Do not fill gaps with random colours, duplicate shades or transparent entries. Use actual opaque black placeholders.
The black squares are layout spacers, not usable game colours. Document them clearly in the accompanying palette guide so I don't accidentally use them as material colours.
If Aseprite cannot reliably distinguish placeholder colours from real colours, include a clear row map or index guide explaining which black entries are spacers. Do not silently replace the black placeholders with other colours.
Suggested grid layout
Aim for approximately 12 columns per row, with a row for each of the following material families:
• Core architecture / violet concrete
• Secondary stone / paving / gravel
• Vegetation / grass / foliage
• Deep vegetation / moss / corrupted growth
• Dirt / soil / paths
• Wood / dead timber
• Metal / gates / railings
• Glass / windows / reflections
• Skin / flesh
• Pale skin / highlights / subtle undertones
• Fabric / uniforms / hospital clothing
• Paper / bone / clinical surfaces
• Blood / injury / restrained narrative accents
• UI darks / text / panel backgrounds
• UI lights / readable text / neutral surfaces
• Unnatural light / mental-state anomalies / distortion
These are suggested families. If some would benefit from merging or splitting, use your judgement, but maintain the strict rectangular layout.
Use a maximum of 12 colour slots per row unless you have a compelling reason to use more. The entire palette should remain manageable, ideally around 120–180 meaningful colours plus the required black spacers.

6. COLOUR DESIGN RULES
Please follow these principles throughout:
Value structure comes first. Each material needs enough difference between its darkest shadow, midtones and highlights to read clearly at small sprite sizes.
Hue relationships must be deliberate. The architecture should lean violet, vegetation should lean jade, and the main warm colours should be muted and controlled.
Bold does not mean every colour is bright. Use a predominantly dark and mid-value world, with lighter colours reserved for readable surfaces and focal points.
Avoid competing accent families. Do not introduce large unrelated families of bright orange, hot pink, electric cyan, red, yellow and lime simultaneously. The main identity is violet and jade, with acid green as the standout accent.
Keep material identity. A player should be able to distinguish grass from concrete, skin from stone, and metal from glass even when the scene is dark.
Support psychological horror through selective violations. In distorted scenes, certain surfaces can break their usual colour rules, but the ordinary palette should remain coherent.
Do not rely on global colour filters. We want the ability to change specific tiles, objects, lights and surfaces intentionally.
